STAT 551 · APPLIED STATISTICAL RESEARCH PROJECT

Alcohol &
health outcomes.

A team research project using statistical learning to explore binge drinking and state-level alcohol-related health outcomes.

Cal Poly StatisticsR · tidymodels · QuartoPresented to Statistics faculty
Source & documentation ↗Original research poster (PDF) ↗

Problem

How do demographic, socioeconomic, and health characteristics relate to binge drinking? At the state level, how do health and income patterns relate to alcohol-related outcomes? We explored these as separate individual-level and aggregate questions.

Data

BRFSS

A 50,000-row survey extract, before cleaning. Predictors included age, income, general health, and other health and demographic responses.

USCDI

U.S. Chronic Disease Indicators, with state-level alcohol/liver indicators. The project summary describes roughly 21,500 indicator records in the analysis.

The saved USCDI input extract contains 50,000 rows. The approximately 21,500-record analysis scope comes from the project summary; its exact filtering stage is not preserved. These counts are not final model sample sizes.

Methods

We built two modeling pipelines in R and tidymodels. The individual-level analysis used elastic net logistic regression and random forests: elastic net identified influential predictors, while random forests captured nonlinear patterns and interactions. The Quarto sources include five-fold cross-validation and ROC AUC tuning.

For the state-level analysis, we aggregated BRFSS measures by state and joined them to USCDI indicators. The poster defines high burden as the top 25% of states by liver mortality and uses elastic net to examine population-level associations.

Results

  • Adults aged 18–34 had the highest reported binge-drinking rates; adults 55 and older had lower rates.
  • More days of poor mental health were associated with a higher likelihood of binge drinking. Lower education and income were associated with slightly higher risk.
  • The poster reports greater state-level mortality burden alongside higher binge-drinking rates, lower income, and lower education. Its conclusion also notes poorer general health in higher-burden areas.
  • After including age, associations with smoking and exercise weakened.

These findings are reported in our original poster, which includes age-group, mental-health, random-forest importance, and state-burden plots. They are observational findings; no unreported effect sizes or performance scores have been added.

Presentation & takeaways

I presented this work to Cal Poly Statistics faculty. The project connected data cleaning, model building, and communicating the limits of an analysis.

This was a team project with Jose Garcia. Our original poster, Alcohol Consumption Trends in the United States, is linked above. The repository preserves my analysis and Jose’s work in Jose.qmd.

Limitations & source materials

These are observational associations. State-level patterns cannot establish individual risk or causation, and chronic liver disease mortality is broader than alcohol-attributable mortality. The saved sources need a coding and evaluation review before rerunning: missing-value handling, outcome labels, an undefined random-forest recipe, and target leakage in one state-level workflow all need attention.
